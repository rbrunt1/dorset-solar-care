Local conditions

Pines, resin and steep Victorian roofs

What actually lands on a roof in Bournemouth, and why it matters.

The pine trees are the difference

Bournemouth is a town built through pine woods and chines, and a great many roofs sit under or beside mature Scots and maritime pines. Needles collect along the bottom edge of an array and in the gap between panel and tile, where they hold moisture against the frame.

Resin does not wash off

Pine resin is the awkward one. It is sticky, it traps everything that lands on it, and it is unaffected by rain. Left through a summer it darkens and bonds to the glass. It needs removing properly rather than hosing.

Steep Victorian and Edwardian pitches

Much of the older housing stock has steep, tall roofs — often three storeys with a pitch well past 40 degrees. That changes how a visit is planned and staged, and it is why we would rather see the roof before quoting anything unusual.

Shade patterns that move through the year

Tree cover that is harmless in December can clip the corner of an array from May onwards. Partial shade matters more than most people expect, for reasons worth understanding.

Our honest take for Bournemouth: If your array is under pines, resin is the thing to watch rather than general grime. It is worth looking at the panels at the end of a hot spell.

What a visit covers

  • • A full clean of the array using deionised water, so nothing is left behind as it dries
  • • A documented inspection: mounting and clamps, cable runs and clips, isolators, and inverter readings
  • • Minor repairs on the spot where they are minor — a loose clip, a sagging cable, a slipped bird guard
  • • A written report each visit, so you have a history of the system rather than a vague memory
  • • A flag on anything that needs a qualified installer, with no attempt to do it ourselves

We are a maintenance and cleaning business, not an MCS-certified installer. If something needs an installer or an electrician, we will tell you plainly rather than have a go.
